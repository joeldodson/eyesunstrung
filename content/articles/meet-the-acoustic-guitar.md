This article describes the guitar one piece at a time, starting at the body and working up to the tuning knobs.
You can read it with a guitar in your lap and follow along by touch.
Or you can read it first, so you know what to expect when you pick one up.
It assumes no musical background.

This description is based on a standard-sized steel-string guitar.
Describing a guitar is awkward because its position keeps changing.
It sits in your lap, hangs on a wall, or rests in a case.
So the directions here describe the guitar itself, and they stay the same in any position.
Picture the guitar hanging by its headstock from a hook, with the front facing you.
The headstock is at the top, the far end of the body is at the bottom, and up and down follow from that.
The front is the face with the soundhole in it, and the back is the opposite side.
The sides connect the front to the back.
The directions for holding the guitar, later in this article, assume it is held as a right-handed person would.

## The Big Picture

An acoustic guitar has three main sections, arranged in a line.

- The body is the large hollow part.
- The neck is the long, narrow part that your fretting hand works along.
- The headstock is the small paddle at the top of the guitar, at the end of the neck, which holds the tuning knobs.

A standard-sized guitar is about forty inches, or a meter, from end to end.
Six strings run nearly the whole length.

## The Body

The body is a hollow wooden box with a curved outline.
It's wide at the bottom, narrow in the middle, and a bit smaller at the top.
The narrow part in the middle is called the waist.

The flat wooden face is the front, though it's also called the top or the soundboard.
Opposite it is the back, and the sides connect the two.
The hollow body is what makes the guitar loud enough to hear.

The soundhole is a round opening in the front, just below where the neck joins the body.
The strings pass directly over it.
You can slip a fingertip inside the edge and feel how deep the body is.

There might be a pickguard, a thin, smooth plastic plate on the front of the guitar, usually next to the soundhole on the side nearest the thin strings.
It protects the wood from picks and fingernails.
Not every guitar has one.

Below the soundhole is the bridge.
It's a rectangular block of wood glued to the front, about six inches, or fifteen centimeters, across.
Two parts of it matter.

- The saddle is a thin, hard strip set into a slot along the bridge, slightly raised, that the strings rest on.
- The bridge pins are six small round pegs in a row, one per string, each holding a string end in place.
  They sit just below the saddle, on the side away from the soundhole.

Leave the bridge pins where they are, since pulling one releases its string.

At the bottom of the body, the end farthest from the neck, is a metal strap button.
Some guitars have a second one near where the neck joins the body.
They're for attaching a strap.

Some guitars have a cutaway, a place where the upper body is scooped away so your hand can reach the highest frets.
If yours has one, you'll feel the body curve inward near the neck.

## The Strings

A standard guitar has six strings.
They're numbered from the thinnest to the thickest.
The first string is the thinnest, and the sixth string is the thickest.
Strings 3, 4, 5, and 6 are wound with fine wire to increase their mass.
Strings 1 and 2 are plain wire.
The first string has the highest pitch, and the sixth has the lowest.

From the sixth string to the first, the strings are tuned to the notes E, A, D, G, B, and E.
Those letters are worth learning early.
A common mnemonic is "Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie."

Steel strings are firm.
Your fingertips will probably be sore for the first week or two of frequent playing.
That passes as the skin toughens.

Each string runs from its bridge pin, over the saddle, across the soundhole, and along the neck toward the headstock.
Follow one of them and you'll arrive at the neck.

## The Neck

The neck is a long piece of wood, rounded on the back and flat on the front.
It joins the body at the 12th or 14th fret, depending on the guitar.
That join is a useful landmark, and worth getting to know early.

The flat front of the neck is the fretboard.
It's a little under two inches, or about four and a half centimeters, wide at the headstock end.
It gets slightly wider toward the body, and your fingers can span it.

Across the fretboard run the frets.
These are thin metal bars set into the wood at right angles to the strings.
A guitar usually has 19 to 21 of them.

Frets are numbered from the headstock end, so the first fret is the one farthest from the body.
The numbers count up as you move toward the body.
The frets are widest apart near the headstock and get closer together as you move toward the body.

When you press a string down on the headstock side of a fret, close to the fret, that string sounds at a higher pitch.
That's how you play different notes.
Moving toward the body gives higher notes.
You'll often hear guitarists call this moving up the neck.
That sounds backward next to the up and down used in this article, because the body is at the bottom, but it refers to rising pitch.

Sighted players use small dots on the fretboard to keep track of where they are.
Those dots are usually flush with the wood, so you probably can't feel them.
I have silicone bump dots on my guitar at the same places as those visual dots.
Ideally, at some point, you'll know where you are on the fretboard by feel and sound.

At the headstock end of the neck is the nut.
It's a small, hard bar, usually white plastic or bone, with six shallow grooves.
Each string passes through its own groove.
The nut marks the start of the playable part of the string.

## The Headstock

The headstock is a flat wooden paddle at the end of the neck.
On most steel-string guitars it carries six tuning machines, three along each edge.
Each one has a small metal post that a string winds around, and a knob that sticks out sideways for turning.
The knob is often referred to as the key.

Turning a knob tightens or loosens its string, and that changes the pitch.
Tuning deserves its own explanation, so leave the knobs alone for now.

The string ends often stick out past the posts.
Cut wire can be sharp, so go slowly around that area.

## Holding the Guitar

Sit in a chair without arms.
Rest the waist of the guitar on your right thigh, with the back of the body against your chest and the front facing away from you.
The neck points to your left and angles slightly upward.

In this position the thickest string is the one nearest your face, and the thinnest is nearest the floor.
That's easy to get backward.
The sixth string is the lowest in pitch, and it's the highest in position.

Rest your right forearm on the edge of the body that faces the ceiling, so your right hand falls over the strings near the soundhole.
That hand plucks or strums.
Cup the neck with your left hand, thumb on the back of the neck and fingertips on the fretboard.
That hand presses the strings, and is called the fretting hand.

A left-handed person might also play this way, or use a left-handed guitar.
A left-handed guitar is held the other way round.
It rests on your left thigh, the neck points to your right, your right hand frets, and your left hand plucks or strums.
The description of the guitar itself is the same.

## Exploring a Guitar by Touch

Rest the guitar flat across your lap, with the front facing up and the neck pointing to your left.
Up and down still describe the guitar, so in this position down is toward your right.

1. Trace the outline of the body with both hands, finding the wide lower section, the waist, and the smaller upper section.
2. Find the soundhole with your fingertips and feel its round edge.
3. Move down to the bridge and find the saddle, then the six pins in a row just below it.
4. Follow one string from the bridge, across the soundhole, and along the neck to the headstock.
5. Find where the neck meets the body, then count a few frets away from it and notice how the spacing changes.
6. Find the nut, then the headstock and the six tuning machines, without turning the knobs.
7. Rest your right palm gently on the front of the body and pluck one string with your other hand, and you'll feel the whole body vibrate.

That vibration is how the guitar works.
A string on its own is quiet.
The front picks up its motion, and the hollow body makes it loud.

## Other Kinds of Acoustic Guitar

The main alternative is the nylon-string guitar, also called a classical guitar.
Its strings are softer and easier on the fingers.
The three thin strings are clear nylon, and the three thick ones are wound with fine wire.

The neck is wider and flatter, and usually joins the body at the 12th fret.
The headstock is slotted: two long openings run through it, from the front to the back, along its length.
The tuning posts run across those openings, and each string winds around its post inside an opening.
The knobs still stick out sideways from the edges.
There are no bridge pins, because the strings are tied to the bridge.

Both types are held and played the same way, so everything about playing applies to either, though a pick is not generally used on a nylon-string guitar.

## Glossary

- Body - the large hollow section of the guitar that makes the sound loud.
- Bridge - the block of wood glued to the front, below the soundhole, that anchors the strings.
- Bridge pins - six small pegs that hold the string ends in the bridge.
- Cutaway - a scooped-out part of the upper body, beside the neck, that lets your hand reach the highest frets.
- Fret - a thin metal bar set across the fretboard, where pressing a string down on its headstock side changes the string's pitch.
- Fretboard - the flat front surface of the neck, where the frets are set.
- Headstock - the end of the neck that holds the tuning machines.
- Key - another name for the knob of a tuning machine.
- Neck - the long narrow section between the body and the headstock.
- Nut - the small grooved bar where the neck meets the headstock.
- Pickguard - a thin plastic plate on the front that protects the wood.
- Saddle - the thin strip set into the bridge that the strings rest on.
- Soundhole - the round opening in the front of the body.
- Strap button - a metal knob on the body for attaching a strap.
- Tuning machines - the geared knobs and posts on the headstock that adjust string tension.
- Waist - the narrow middle part of the body.
