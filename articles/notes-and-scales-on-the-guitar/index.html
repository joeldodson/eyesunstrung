Notes and Scales on the Guitar

Published November 19, 2026

This article introduces the notes used in Western music and shows how to find them on a guitar. It covers the names of the notes, the notes the open strings play, how to work out the note at any fret, and enough about major and minor scales to play one along a single string. It assumes no musical background. It does assume you know the parts of the guitar, such as the strings, frets, and headstock, and that the strings are numbered from the thinnest, the first, to the thickest, the sixth. Meet the Acoustic Guitar covers all of that.

If you want to know why notes and scales work the way they do, Physics, Math, and Music goes into the science behind them. Read it if you are interested in the physics and math behind Western music. Don't be intimidated by it, though. You can play fine without knowing most of what is in there.

Notes and Pitch

A note is a single musical sound with a definite pitch. Pitch is how high or low a sound is. On a guitar, the thick strings have a lower pitch and the thin strings a higher one.

Throughout this article, higher and lower mean pitch, not position.

The Musical Alphabet

Western music names its notes with the first seven letters of the alphabet: A, B, C, D, E, F, and G. After G, the letters start again at A.

The A you arrive at after G is not the same pitch as the A you started from. It's higher, but it sounds like a higher version of the same note. The distance from one note to the next note with the same name is called an octave. The name comes from the Latin for eight, because counting both ends, there are eight letters from one A to the next: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A.

Half Steps and Whole Steps

The distance between two notes is measured in steps. The smallest step in Western music is the half step, also called a semitone. Two half steps make a whole step, also called a whole tone.

On a guitar, a half step is one fret. Press a string at one fret, then at the next fret toward the body, and the note rises by a half step. A whole step is two frets.

Between most neighboring letters there is a whole step. There are two exceptions.

  • B to C is a half step.
  • E to F is a half step.

Every other pair of neighbors, A to B, C to D, D to E, F to G, and G to A, is a whole step. That's the most useful single fact for finding notes on the guitar, and it's worth memorizing now.

Sharps and Flats

A whole step leaves room for one note in between. That note is named after its neighbors, using a sharp or a flat.

  • Sharp means one half step higher.
  • Flat means one half step lower.

So the note between A and B is A sharp, because it's a half step higher than A. It's also B flat, because it's a half step lower than B. These are two names for the same note, and the guitar plays them at the same fret. Which name gets used depends on the scale it belongs to, as the section on the major scale explains later in this article.

Sharps and flats are called accidentals. A note named with a plain letter, with no sharp or flat, is called a natural.

There's no note between B and C, or between E and F, because they're already only a half step apart. You'll sometimes meet names such as E sharp or C flat. E sharp is another name for F, and C flat is another name for B. They come up in some written music, and you can leave them aside for now.

In print, the sharp sign is the same character as the hash or number sign, and the flat sign is a lowercase b. So a screen reader may read C# as "C number" or "C hash", and Bb as "B b". Now that you know what the signs mean, you'll recognize them in chord charts and song sheets.

The Twelve Notes

Put the naturals, sharps, and flats together and there are twelve different notes. Starting from A, each a half step higher than the one before, they are:

  1. A
  2. A sharp, or B flat
  3. B
  4. C
  5. C sharp, or D flat
  6. D
  7. D sharp, or E flat
  8. E
  9. F
  10. F sharp, or G flat
  11. G
  12. G sharp, or A flat

After G sharp comes A again, an octave above where you started. Twelve half steps make one octave. All twelve notes played in order, a half step apart, are called the chromatic scale.

The Open Strings

An open string is a string played without pressing it down at any fret. In standard tuning, the most common tuning, the open strings play these notes.

  • Sixth string, the thickest - E
  • Fifth string - A
  • Fourth string - D
  • Third string - G
  • Second string - B
  • First string, the thinnest - E

The sixth and first strings are both E, two octaves apart. Guitarists call them the low E and the high E. A common mnemonic for the order, from the sixth string to the first, is "Eddie Ate Dynamite, Good Bye Eddie."

These six notes are your starting points. Every other note on the guitar can be found by counting frets from one of them.

Finding Notes Along a String

Each fret raises the pitch of a string by a half step. Frets are numbered from the headstock end, so fret 1 is the one nearest the headstock. Pressing a string at a fret means holding it against the fretboard just on the headstock side of that fret, as described in the neck section of Meet the Acoustic Guitar.

To find the note at a fret, start from the open string's note and count through the twelve notes, one half step per fret. Remember that B to C and E to F are only one fret apart.

The Low E String

These are the notes on the sixth string, from the open string to the 12th fret.

  • Open - E
  • Fret 1 - F
  • Fret 2 - F sharp, or G flat
  • Fret 3 - G
  • Fret 4 - G sharp, or A flat
  • Fret 5 - A
  • Fret 6 - A sharp, or B flat
  • Fret 7 - B
  • Fret 8 - C
  • Fret 9 - C sharp, or D flat
  • Fret 10 - D
  • Fret 11 - D sharp, or E flat
  • Fret 12 - E

Fret 1 is F, only a half step above the open E. Fret 8 is C, only a half step above the B at fret 7.

The 12th fret is E again, an octave above the open string. That's true on every string: the 12th fret plays the open string's note an octave higher. Past the 12th fret the pattern repeats, so the 13th fret plays the same note as the 1st, an octave higher. That makes the 12th fret a useful landmark.

The high E string has the same note names at every fret, two octaves higher.

The A String

The fifth string starts on A. These are its naturals, from the open string to the 12th fret.

  • Open - A
  • Fret 2 - B
  • Fret 3 - C
  • Fret 5 - D
  • Fret 7 - E
  • Fret 8 - F
  • Fret 10 - G
  • Fret 12 - A

The frets left out play the sharps and flats. Fret 1 is A sharp or B flat, fret 4 is C sharp or D flat, and so on.

You can work out the fourth, third, and second strings the same way, starting from D, G, and B.

From One String to the Next

There's a shortcut that connects the strings. On the sixth string, fret 5 is A, which is the note of the open fifth string. In the same way, fret 5 of most strings plays the same note as the next thinner string played open.

  • Sixth string, fret 5 - A, the same as the open fifth string.
  • Fifth string, fret 5 - D, the same as the open fourth string.
  • Fourth string, fret 5 - G, the same as the open third string.
  • Third string, fret 4 - B, the same as the open second string.
  • Second string, fret 5 - E, the same as the open first string.

The exception is the third string, where the match is at fret 4 instead of fret 5. Physics, Math, and Music explains why standard tuning has that one odd gap. For now, remember that the third string is the odd one out.

This means most notes can be played in more than one place. The D at fret 10 of the sixth string is the same pitch as the D at fret 5 of the fifth string, and the same as the open fourth string. It's also a way to check your tuning, by comparing a fretted note with the open string it should match.

Precise Note Names

You've seen several times that the same note name comes back at the end of an octave. String 1 is tuned two octaves above string 6, and both are the note E. So if you ask someone to play an E on the guitar, there are several places to play it. To name one exact note, a number follows the letter. This is called scientific pitch notation.

For example, open string 6 is tuned to E2. Open string 1, two octaves higher, is E4.

The number goes up by one at each C, not at each A. So the natural notes rising from the open sixth string are E2, F2, G2, A2, B2, then C3, D3, E3, and so on. A sharp or flat takes the number of its letter, so C sharp 3 is a half step above C3.

The open strings, more precisely, are:

  • Sixth string - E2
  • Fifth string - A2
  • Fourth string - D3
  • Third string - G3
  • Second string - B3
  • First string - E4

The fourth string is D3, not D2, because the number went up at the C between A2 and D3.

The numbers also show which notes from earlier in this article are the same pitch. Fret 5 of the sixth string and the open fifth string are both A2. E3 is at fret 12 of the sixth string, fret 7 of the fifth string, and fret 2 of the fourth string. So the three E's covered so far are E2 on the open sixth string, E3 in the middle, and E4 on the open first string.

Musical Scales

A scale is a set of notes played in order, from a starting note to the same note an octave higher. Most melodies and chords are built from the notes of a scale. The starting note is called the root, and it gives the scale its name, so a major scale starting on G is the G major scale.

The major and minor scales use seven of the twelve notes, one for each letter. What makes one scale different from another is the pattern of half steps and whole steps between its notes. On a single string, that pattern tells you when to move two frets and when to move one.

If you can sing "doh, ray, me, fa, soh, lah, tee, doh", you already know the sound of a major scale.

The Major Scale

The major scale's pattern, from the root, is:

Whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half.

On a single string, that means moving two frets, two frets, one fret, two, two, two, and one. The steps add up to twelve half steps, which is one octave.

Playing a Major Scale on One String

Any open string plays its own major scale at the open string and frets 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 11, and 12. Try it on the fifth string, which gives the A major scale.

  1. Play the open fifth string, A.
  2. Play fret 2, B, a whole step higher.
  3. Play fret 4, C sharp, another whole step.
  4. Play fret 5, D, a half step.
  5. Play fret 7, E, a whole step.
  6. Play fret 9, F sharp, a whole step.
  7. Play fret 11, G sharp, a whole step.
  8. Play fret 12, A, a half step, which brings you back to the root an octave higher.

Then play the same frets in reverse order, from fret 12 back to the open string, to hear the scale falling.

Naming the Notes in a Scale

Each letter appears once in a major scale, in order. That's what decides whether a note is called a sharp or a flat. In A major, the note at fret 9 is called F sharp rather than G flat, because calling it G flat would leave the scale with two G's and no F.

The C major scale is the one with no sharps or flats at all: C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C. Its two half steps fall between E and F and between B and C, exactly where the plain letters already have them. On the second string, the C major scale starts at fret 1 and uses frets 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 13.

Starting From Any Fret

The pattern works from any starting fret, not only from an open string. Find the root on a string, then move along the string toward the body: two frets, two, one, two, two, two, one. For example, the G major scale on the sixth string starts at fret 3, which is G, and uses frets 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, 12, 14, and 15.

Past the 12th fret the frets are close together and near the body, so they can be harder to reach on some guitars.

The Minor Scale

There are several minor scales. The one in this article is the natural minor scale, the most basic of them. Its pattern, from the root, is:

Whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole.

On a single string, that means moving two frets, one fret, two, two, one, two, and two.

Playing a Minor Scale on One String

Any open string plays its own natural minor scale at the open string and frets 2, 3, 5, 7, 8, 10, and 12. On the fifth string, that's the A minor scale.

  1. Play the open fifth string, A.
  2. Play fret 2, B, a whole step higher.
  3. Play fret 3, C, a half step.
  4. Play fret 5, D, a whole step.
  5. Play fret 7, E, a whole step.
  6. Play fret 8, F, a half step.
  7. Play fret 10, G, a whole step.
  8. Play fret 12, A, a whole step, which brings you back to the root an octave higher.

The A minor scale has no sharps or flats. It uses the same seven notes as C major, starting from A instead of C.

The pattern moves to any starting fret in the same way as the major scale's. For example, the B minor scale on the fifth string starts at fret 2, which is B, and uses frets 2, 4, 5, 7, 9, 10, 12, and 14.

How Major and Minor Differ

Compare the A major and A minor scales on the fifth string, note by note.

  • The root - the open string in both.
  • The second note - fret 2 in both.
  • The third note - fret 4 in major, fret 3 in minor.
  • The fourth and fifth notes - frets 5 and 7 in both.
  • The sixth note - fret 9 in major, fret 8 in minor.
  • The seventh note - fret 11 in major, fret 10 in minor.
  • The root an octave higher - fret 12 in both.

The third, sixth, and seventh notes are each a half step lower in minor. The third is the most important of these, and it's where the two scales get their names. Most listeners hear the major scale as bright and settled, and the minor scale as darker or sadder. Play one after the other on the same string and listen for the difference.

Glossary

  • Accidental - a sharp or a flat.
  • Chromatic scale - all twelve notes played in order, a half step apart.
  • Flat - one half step lower, so B flat is a half step lower than B.
  • Half step - the smallest step in Western music, one fret on a guitar, also called a semitone.
  • Major scale - a seven-note scale with the pattern whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half.
  • Musical alphabet - the seven letters, A to G, used to name notes.
  • Natural - a note named with a plain letter, with no sharp or flat.
  • Natural minor scale - a seven-note scale with the pattern whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole.
  • Note - a single musical sound with a definite pitch.
  • Octave - the distance from a note to the next note with the same name, twelve half steps.
  • Open string - a string played without pressing it at any fret.
  • Pitch - how high or low a sound is.
  • Root - the note a scale starts and ends on, which gives the scale its name.
  • Scale - a set of notes played in order from a root to the root an octave higher.
  • Scientific pitch notation - naming one exact note with its letter and an octave number, such as E2 for the open sixth string.
  • Semitone - another name for a half step.
  • Sharp - one half step higher, so A sharp is a half step higher than A.
  • Standard tuning - the most common guitar tuning, E, A, D, G, B, E from the sixth string to the first.
  • Whole step - two half steps, two frets on a guitar, also called a whole tone.
  • Whole tone - another name for a whole step.
